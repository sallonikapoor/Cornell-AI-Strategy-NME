WEEK 03 / PROMPTING

Say exactly what you want.

Write prompts that give you consistent, usable answers, in a format your team and your software can rely on.

About 45 minutesSelf-pacedNothing to submit

Last week you learned to turn a vague request into a clear problem and a small first version. Often, that first version includes a model doing one specific job, like drafting a reply or pulling details out of a document. This week is about getting it to do that job well, the same way, every time.

In Week 1 you saw that a model's behavior comes from three layers, and only one of them is yours: the prompt. You also ran the same prompt five times and may have gotten different answers. By the end of this lesson you'll know why that happens and what to do about it.

You'll need any AI chat tool for the two practice prompts near the end. Use only the made-up examples on this page, or invent your own. Never paste client information into a consumer AI tool, even to test a prompt.

Start with the brief →
01 / THE BRIEF

Brief it like a new hire.

The model knows a lot about the world and nothing about your situation.

When you hand a task to someone on their first day, you tell them who the work is for, what a good result looks like, and what to do when something doesn't fit. A model needs the same things, for the same reason. It starts every conversation knowing nothing about your client, your goal, or what happened yesterday.

Remember the two things a model knows from Week 1: what it learned in training, and what you put in front of it. The prompt is most of the second one.

The new-hire test

Before you send a prompt, picture a smart person who started today and has nothing but your prompt to go on. Could they do the task the way you want? If they'd need to ask you questions first, the model needs those answers too. It just won't ask. It will guess.

The prompt:

Write a reply to this refund email.

What a new hire would ask you:

  • What's our refund policy?
  • What's today's date, and when is the show?
  • Am I allowed to approve exceptions?
  • How long should it be, and how do we sign off?
  • Is there a reply you've liked before?

Each of those questions points to a part of a good prompt. That's the next section.

See the five parts →
02 / BUILD A PROMPT

Five things a good prompt covers.

Not every prompt needs all five, but most weak prompts are missing at least one.

Context.

Who the work is for, what's going on, and any facts it needs, like a policy or today's date.

The task.

The one thing you want done, written as a direct instruction.

Rules.

What it must never do, and what to do when it isn't sure.

Format.

The shape of the answer: how long, how it's organized, and what comes first.

Examples.

A sample of a good answer, for when describing it in words isn't enough.

You'll see prompts that start with "You are a world-class expert." A line like that does little on its own. Specific facts about the situation almost always help more.

Watch one prompt improve

The Linden Room is the made-up music venue from Week 2. Its box office wants a model to draft replies to refund emails, and a staff member reads every draft before it goes out. Here's the email, then five versions of the prompt. Each version adds one part.

Hi, I bought 2 tickets for the March 14 show (order #LR-20418) but I can't make it anymore because of a family emergency. Can I get my money back? Thanks, Priya

Version 3. Add rules

Rules

Prompt
You're drafting email replies for the box office at The Linden Room, a small music venue. A staff member reads every draft before it's sent. Today's date is March 10, 2027.

Our refund policy: Tickets can be refunded up to 7 days before the show, minus a $4 fee per ticket. Within 7 days of the show, tickets can't be refunded, but they can be moved to another show once. Exceptions for emergencies or illness are decided by the box office manager.

Rules:
- Only describe what the policy allows. Never approve an exception or state a refund amount; staff confirm amounts.
- If the request falls outside the policy, explain what the policy does allow and say the box office manager will review their situation within one business day.
- If the order number is missing, ask for it.
- Don't add any details that aren't in the email or the policy.

Write a reply to this refund email.

[email]
Reply
Subject: Re: Refund Request for Order #LR-20418

Dear Priya,

Thank you so much for reaching out to us, and please accept our sincere sympathies regarding your family emergency. We completely understand that unexpected situations arise.

We've reviewed your request for order #LR-20418. Because the March 14 show is less than 7 days away, your tickets fall outside our standard refund window. However, you are welcome to transfer your tickets to another show of your choice.

We also want you to know that our box office manager reviews special circumstances such as yours. They will be in touch within one business day to discuss your options.

What changed

The content is right now. The shape isn't. Staff don't need a subject line, it's three times longer than it should be, and the sign-off doesn't match the one the box office uses. Run it again and you'll get a different shape again.

Version 4. Add format

Format

Prompt
You're drafting email replies for the box office at The Linden Room, a small music venue. A staff member reads every draft before it's sent. Today's date is March 10, 2027.

Our refund policy: Tickets can be refunded up to 7 days before the show, minus a $4 fee per ticket. Within 7 days of the show, tickets can't be refunded, but they can be moved to another show once. Exceptions for emergencies or illness are decided by the box office manager.

Rules:
- Only describe what the policy allows. Never approve an exception or state a refund amount; staff confirm amounts.
- If the request falls outside the policy, explain what the policy does allow and say the box office manager will review their situation within one business day.
- If the order number is missing, ask for it.
- Don't add any details that aren't in the email or the policy.

Format: Plain text, no subject line, under 100 words. Start with "Hi" and their first name. Then two or three sentences on what the policy allows for their situation, and one sentence on the next step. Sign off as "The Linden Room Box Office".

Look back at what fixed each version. It wasn't clever wording. It was facts, limits, and a clear picture of the finished product. Good prompts are mostly clear thinking, written down.

Why answers change →
03 / CONSISTENCY

When the answer keeps changing, check the question.

Inconsistent answers usually point to a task with more than one reasonable answer.

In Week 1's consistency check, you ran this prompt five times, each in a new chat:

Classify this deduction into exactly one category (PRICING, SHORTAGE, DAMAGE, or PROMOTION). Respond with only the category. Deduction note: 'Customer deducted $2,340 citing 14 cases received crushed and short-shipped on PO 44817; promo price also not applied.'

Some variation comes from the model itself. It chooses among likely next tokens, so the wording of a reply shifts from run to run. But when the actual answer changes, like a category or a yes or no, the cause is usually the task. That note describes three problems at once, and the prompt demanded exactly one category. There was no right answer, so the model guessed, and it guessed differently each time.

A better version

Classify this retail deduction into one category.

PRICING: the customer paid less because they disagree with the price we billed.
SHORTAGE: fewer units arrived than we billed for.
DAMAGE: units arrived unusable.
PROMOTION: a promotional price or allowance we agreed to wasn't applied.
MULTIPLE: more than one category applies.

If the note doesn't give you enough to decide, answer NEEDS_REVIEW.
Respond with only the category name.

Deduction note: 'Customer deducted $2,340 citing 14 cases received crushed and short-shipped on PO 44817; promo price also not applied.'

Now the right answer is MULTIPLE, and a model will give it reliably. Three changes did the work:

  • Definitions. Each category says what it covers, so PRICING and PROMOTION stop overlapping.
  • A label for the hard case. MULTIPLE gives notes like this one a correct answer to land on.
  • A way to say "I can't tell." NEEDS_REVIEW lets the model flag a note instead of picking something that merely sounds right.

Examples help at the edges

Definitions get you most of the way. For the cases that sit right on a boundary, a couple of labeled examples help more than another paragraph of description:

Examples:
Note: "Short 3 cases on PO 51120" → SHORTAGE
Note: "Billed $18.40/case, agreed price was $17.90" → PRICING

Pick examples that are close calls, not obvious ones, and make them different from each other. If every example looks alike, the model learns to copy them instead of learning the rule.

Where this goes in a real workflow

MULTIPLE and NEEDS_REVIEW are exactly the cases you'd send to a person. That's the Week 1 capability sort in action: the model handles the clear-cut notes, and a human handles the ambiguous ones.

Developer tools also let you turn down a model's randomness, which you'll see in Week 6. That helps, but it won't fix an unclear task. A model that gives the same wrong answer every time is consistent, but it isn't useful.

Ask for a form →
04 / STRUCTURED OUTPUT

Ask for a form, not an essay.

When the answer feeds a spreadsheet or another program, its shape matters as much as its content.

A drafted reply is written for a person to read. But a lot of client work ends with the output going somewhere else: a row in a spreadsheet, a ticket in a queue, the input to the next step. Software is much less forgiving than people are. If one answer says "2 tickets" and the next says "two," a person reads both just fine, and a spreadsheet chokes.

The usual fix is to ask for JSON, a plain-text format made of labeled fields. You don't need to write code to use it. Here are the details from Priya's email, as JSON:

{
  "customer_first_name": "Priya",
  "order_number": "LR-20418",
  "show_date": "2027-03-14",
  "ticket_count": 2,
  "reason": "family emergency",
  "request_type": "refund"
}

Each line is a label and a value. Text goes in quotes, numbers don't, and null means "not provided."

The prompt that produced it

Read the refund email below and fill in these fields. Return only the JSON, with no other text before or after it.

customer_first_name: text
order_number: exactly as written in the email
show_date: YYYY-MM-DD
ticket_count: a whole number
reason: a short phrase in the customer's own words
request_type: one of "refund", "transfer", or "other"

If the email doesn't state something, use null. Don't guess.

[email]

What makes it work

  • Name every field, spelled exactly the way your spreadsheet or software expects.
  • Say what each value should look like: a date as YYYY-MM-DD, a whole number, or one choice from a fixed list.
  • Say what to do when something's missing. Here, that's null. Without that rule, the model fills the gap with something plausible.
  • Ask for only the JSON. Anything before or after it, even "Sure! Here you go," can break the next step.

Leave the math to code

Notice that the prompt doesn't ask whether Priya's request falls within the refund window. That's date arithmetic, and Week 1 showed why exact calculations belong in code. The model pulls out the show date. A few lines of code compare it to today.

Many AI platforms now have a setting that forces the output to match the fields you define, which takes care of problems like A and C. You'll use one in Week 6. Problem D never goes away by itself. A perfectly formatted answer can still be wrong, so check values against something real, and keep a person on anything that matters.

Test before you trust →
05 / TESTING

One good answer proves very little.

Before you trust a prompt, run it on a set of cases where you already know the right answer.

A prompt that works on the email you wrote it for has passed one test, and it was the easiest one. Real inputs are messier: missing details, two requests in one message, an angry customer, a typo in the order number. The only way to know how your prompt handles them is to try them on purpose.

How to test a prompt

  1. Collect 10 to 20 sample inputs. Include easy ones, tricky ones, and a few that are just strange.
  2. Before running anything, write down what a right answer looks like for each one.
  3. Run every case and compare. Mark each one pass or fail, and note why it failed.
  4. Change one thing in the prompt, then rerun every case, not just the one that failed. A fix for one case can quietly break another.

Keep a short log

This habit is called evaluation, or "evals," and you'll come back to it in Week 8. For now the idea is simple: decide what a right answer looks like before you look at the output, so the output can't talk you into it.

For client work, test cases should come from the client, with their permission, through tools the engagement has approved. For practice, invent them.

Try it yourself →
06 / PRACTICE

Find the missing piece.

Each prompt below is missing one part. Pick it, then see why.

0 of 4 correct

Prompt 1

Summarize this 40-page vendor contract.

Context

Summarize it for whom, and for what decision? A summary for a lawyer checking risk looks nothing like one for a manager deciding whether to renew. Say who's reading and what they need to decide, and the summary gets much more useful. (A length limit would help too.)

Prompt 2

Pull the invoice number, date, and total from each of these 200 invoices.

Format

With 200 results, the shape is everything. Name the fields exactly, say how dates and amounts should be written, ask for one row or JSON object per invoice, and say what to use when a value is missing.

Prompt 3

You're helping the front desk at a gym reply to members who want to cancel. Our policy: cancellations take effect at the end of the current billing month, with 30 days' notice. Reply to this message: "I'm moving next week, cancel my membership and refund this month."

Rules

The context is there, but nothing says what the model must not do. Without rules, it may promise a refund or waive the notice period to be helpful. Tell it to describe only what the policy allows, never to offer exceptions, and to pass special requests to a staff member.

Prompt 4

We're a neighborhood coffee shop. Write three Instagram captions announcing our new fall menu, each under 30 words, in our brand voice.

Example

"Our brand voice" means something to you and nothing to the model. Describing a voice in words rarely works. Pasting in two captions you've liked does.

What to look for +

There's no order number, so the reply should ask for it. The takeaway →
07 / THE TAKEAWAY

How do you get the same good answer twice?

Brief the model like a new hire. Give it the context, one clear task, rules for what it must never do and what to do when it's unsure, and the exact format you need. Add an example when words aren't enough. If the answers keep changing, look for ambiguity in the task before you blame the model. When the output feeds software, ask for labeled fields and leave the math to code. And before you trust any prompt, test it on cases where you already know the right answer.

Common prompting mistakes

Asking for three things at once.

Split them into separate prompts or steps.

No plan for "I'm not sure."

Give the model a way to say it can't tell, or it will guess.

"Keep it short."

Say how short: under 100 words, or three bullets.

Testing on one example.

The email you wrote the prompt for is the easiest case you'll ever see.

Trusting a tidy format.

Neat fields can still hold a wrong value.

Check your understanding

You run a prompt five times and get three different answers. What do you check first? +

Whether the task itself is ambiguous. Look for inputs that fit more than one answer, add a rule or a label for those cases, and give the model a way to say it isn't sure.

Why tell the model what to do when information is missing? +

Otherwise it fills the gap with something that sounds right. Telling it to use null, or to ask for the missing detail, turns a hidden guess into something a person can see and fix.

The model returned perfectly formatted JSON. Is the content correct? +

Not necessarily. Format and accuracy are separate. Code can check what's checkable, like whether the order number exists or the totals add up, and a person reviews anything that matters and can't be checked automatically.

When is an example worth adding to a prompt? +

When describing the answer in words isn't enough, like a brand voice or a close call between two categories. Use examples that differ from each other, so the model learns the pattern instead of copying one answer.
