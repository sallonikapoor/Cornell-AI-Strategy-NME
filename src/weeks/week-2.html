WEEK 02 / SCOPING

Find the real problem. Then scope it.

Turn a vague client request into a clear problem, a measurable goal, and a first version you can deliver.

Last week you decided who or what should handle each step of a workflow. But on a real engagement, nobody hands you the workflow. A client says something like 'we want AI to handle our emails,' and it's on you to figure out what's actually going wrong, what success would look like, and what to build first. That's scoping. Get it wrong and you can build something that works perfectly and still doesn't help.

Start with the request →
01 / FROM REQUEST TO PROBLEM

The request is a guess.

Clients describe the solution they imagine. Your job is to find the problem underneath it.

When a client asks for something specific, they're usually guessing at a fix for a problem they feel but haven't pinned down. That's normal. Your job is to work backward from what they asked for to what's actually going wrong.

We want an AI chatbot for our website.

Staff spend hours answering the same few questions by email.

Can you build us a dashboard?

Leadership doesn't trust the numbers they get today.

Use AI to write our weekly reports.

Reports take two people a full day every week, and half the time goes to collecting data.

Notice that the real problem points to different solutions than the request did. If leadership doesn't trust the numbers, a prettier dashboard won't help. If report-writing time goes into collecting data, a better writing tool only fixes half of it.

The problem statement

Once you've found the problem, write it down in one sentence. This formula keeps it specific:

[Who] struggles with [what] because [root cause], which costs [impact].

The box office staff spend about 10 hours a week on refund emails because every request is handled by hand, which delays refunds and leads to occasional math mistakes.

If you can't fill in the 'because,' you don't understand the problem yet. That's what discovery is for.

Learn to ask the right questions →
02 / DISCOVERY

Ask about what actually happens.

Good discovery questions get you specifics: real examples, real numbers, and where the data lives.

Discovery is the first conversation with a client, where you learn how things work today. The goal is to understand the problem well enough to write that problem statement, before you suggest any solution.

What to ask about

The process today.

Walk me through the last time this happened.

Who does each step?

Volume and time.

How often does this happen in a week?

How long does each one take?

The pain.

What's the most frustrating part?

What does it cost you when it goes wrong?

The data.

Where does this information live today?

Can we get a sample?

Success.

If this were fixed, what would be different?

How would you know?

Limits.

What can't go wrong?

What's your budget, and who would maintain this?

Habits that make discovery work

  • Ask about the last time, not "usually." Real examples are more accurate than general descriptions.
  • Get numbers. "A lot of emails" becomes "about 120 a week."
  • Find where the data lives. An AI tool can only use information that exists somewhere it can reach.
  • Find who feels the pain. The person who asked isn't always the person most affected.
  • Hold off on solutions. Once you start pitching, the client stops telling you things.
  • Summarize it back. "So the main problem is… did I get that right?"

That third habit connects straight back to Week 1: a model only knows what you put in front of it. Discovery is where you find out whether that information even exists.

03 / PRACTICE

You get five questions.

Interview a made-up client, then see what a strong consultant would have found.

The brief

Copperline Bikes is a made-up bike shop with two locations and a repair service. The owner sends CAIS this message:

Hi! We're Copperline Bikes. We get way too many customer emails and we want to use AI to answer them. Can you build us a chatbot? — Owner, Copperline Bikes.

You have time for five questions before the owner has to get back to the shop. Pick the five you think will teach you the most, then read the owner's answers. Choose carefully: some questions are much more useful than others.

0 of 5 questions asked

the owner: Whoever's at the counter checks the shared inbox between customers, usually me or Sam. If it's about a repair, we walk to the back and ask the mechanics.

the owner: About 120 a week. I'd guess more than half are 'is my bike ready yet?' The rest are hours, prices, and the occasional complaint.

the owner: On a whiteboard in the back. The mechanics update it… usually. Sometimes it's a day behind.

the owner: Every 'is it ready' email means interrupting a mechanic. They hate it, and it slows down repairs. That's honestly what bugs me most.

the owner: My mechanics wouldn't get interrupted all day. And customers wouldn't have to email twice because we were slow to answer.

the owner: Prices. Last year someone was told $40 for a repair and charged $120. We got a one-star review that still shows up first.

the owner: We use Square for payments and appointments. I don't want to pay more than about $50 a month for anything new. And nobody here is technical, so it has to be simple.

the owner: Sure, everything's in our Gmail going back two years.

the owner: I have no idea. Isn't that your job?

the owner: Sure, I guess? I just want fewer emails.

Your questions

    What a strong consultant would have found

    Put the owner's answers together and the real problem isn't 'too many emails.' More than half the emails are one question: 'is my bike ready?' And nobody can answer that quickly, because repair status lives on a whiteboard in the back that's sometimes a day out of date.

    Copperline's mechanics get interrupted dozens of times a week by repair-status questions because status lives on a whiteboard no one else can see, which slows down repairs and frustrates customers.

    Here's the catch: a chatbot can't answer 'is my bike ready?' either. The information isn't written down anywhere it can reach. Building the chatbot the owner asked for would just move the problem.

    A better first scope

    Phase 1: Get the data in order.

    Track repair status digitally (Square or a simple shared spreadsheet) instead of on the whiteboard. Code sends customers an automatic text or email when their bike's status changes.

    Phase 2: Help with the other emails.

    An LLM drafts replies to questions about hours and general info, and staff review them before they're sent. It never quotes prices; it links to the posted price list instead.

    Out of scope for now:

    A public chatbot.

    What “done” looks like

    Cut 'is it ready' emails by half within a month, measured by tagging emails in the inbox for two weeks before and after. Guardrail: zero wrong price quotes.

    Notice that the most valuable first step isn't AI at all. That happens a lot. Sometimes the best scope fixes how information is tracked, and the AI comes second.

    Bonus: Practice with an AI client

    Want more practice? Have an AI play the client. Paste this into any AI tool, then interview it.

    Interview an AI client

    You are the owner of Copperline Bikes, a made-up two-location bike shop with a repair service. If the consultant asks your name, make one up. A student consultant is interviewing you. You asked them to "build a chatbot" because you get too many customer emails.

Only reveal these facts when the consultant asks a question that would reasonably lead to them. Never volunteer them:
- About 120 emails a week; more than half ask whether a repair is done.
- Repair status is tracked on a whiteboard in the back, often a day behind.
- Your biggest frustration: mechanics get interrupted constantly.
- Success to you: mechanics aren't interrupted, and customers don't email twice.
- Must never happen: quoting a wrong price (a bad review last year).
- You use Square; budget about $50/month; nobody on staff is technical.
- Two years of emails are in Gmail.

Answer like a busy, friendly shop owner, in 1–3 sentences. If asked about AI models or technical details, say you don't know and that's why you hired them. After the consultant says "end interview," tell them which important facts they never uncovered.

    What to look for +

    Did you uncover the whiteboard? It's the key fact, and it only comes out if you ask where repair status lives. If you didn't find it, notice which question would have gotten you there.

    Define what done looks like →
    04 / DEFINING DONE

    "Better" isn't a goal.

    A success metric says what you'll measure, where it starts, where it should end up, and what must not get worse.

    Vague:

    Make refund handling faster.

    Clear:

    Cut the average time to resolve a refund request from 3 days to 1 day within the first month, measured from the email timestamp to the refund confirmation. Guardrail: no refund exceeds what the policy allows.

    Time to resolve means the elapsed time a customer waits, including time in a queue. Staff time means the minutes someone actively spends on the request. Choose the measure that matches the problem you want to solve.

    The clear version has five parts. Leave any out and you'll have trouble proving the project worked.

    • Metric: what you measure (time to resolve a refund).
    • Baseline: where it is today (3 days).
    • Target: where it should end up, and by when (1 day, within a month).
    • How it's measured: where the numbers come from (email and refund timestamps).
    • Guardrail: what must not get worse while you improve the metric (no over-policy refunds).

    The guardrail is the one people forget, and it ties back to Week 1. Speeding up a process with AI is easy if you stop checking the work. The guardrail makes sure you didn't.

    Don't know the baseline yet? That's fine. Measuring it becomes the first task. You can't show improvement without a starting point.

    Draw the boundaries →
    05 / SCOPE

    Decide what you won't build.

    A clear scope says what's in the first version, what's out, and what could come later.

    Client projects grow. Every meeting adds a 'could it also…?' Writing down what's in, out, and later protects your team's time and keeps the client's expectations realistic. It also makes 'not in this phase' an easy thing to say.

    The Linden Room: refund requests

    The Linden Room is a made-up music venue. Its box office staff handle refund requests by email. Here is a first version that helps staff process those requests while keeping approval with a person.

    In (first version):

    Read refund emails, check the policy, calculate amounts, and draft replies for staff to review.

    Out:

    Automatic approval of any refund. Refund requests by phone.

    Later:

    Connecting directly to the ticketing platform so staff don't copy order details by hand.

    Assumptions and risks

    Every scope rests on things you're assuming are true. Write them down, so that if one turns out to be false, everyone knows why the plan changed.

    Assumptions:

    The ticketing system can export order data. Staff will review drafted replies within a day.

    Risks:

    Some emails won't include enough information to find the order. Special-circumstance requests may be more common than expected.

    Common scoping mistakes

    Solving the request, not the problem.

    Building the chatbot the owner asked for.

    Vague success.

    "Faster" or "better," with no number attached.

    Too big a first version.

    Trying to automate the whole process at once.

    Assuming the data exists.

    Discovering mid-project that it's on a whiteboard.

    No guardrail.

    Hitting the speed target while errors quietly go up.

    Title

    The Linden Room: Refund Requests, Scoping Brief

    This is a fictional planning example. The volumes, timings, and suspected cause below are assumptions to check during discovery.

    The request

    The Linden Room is a made-up music venue. Its box office staff read refund emails, find orders, check the refund policy, calculate amounts, and reply by hand. The owner asks: “Can AI just handle our refund emails? We’re drowning.”

    The real problem

    The box office staff spend about 10 hours a week on refund emails because every request is handled by hand, which delays refunds by several days and leads to occasional fee mistakes.

    Discovery questions

    (starred = most important):

    • ★ "Walk me through the last refund request you handled." To see the real steps and where time goes.
    • ★ "How many requests come in a week, and how long does each take?" To size the problem. (About 50 a week, roughly 12 minutes each.)
    • ★ "Where do order details live, and can we export them?" Without order data, nothing can be automated.
    • "How often do requests fall outside the policy?" Exceptions need a person, so their volume shapes the design.
    • "What mistakes have happened before?" To find what the guardrail should protect.
    • "Who approves large refunds today?" To keep the existing sign-off in place.
    • "What would make this a success for you?" To align on the goal before proposing anything.

    What done looks like

    Cut the average time to resolve a refund from 3 days to 1 day within a month, measured from the email timestamp to the refund confirmation. Cut staff time on refunds from an assumed 10 hours a week to under 4 within the same month, measured with a staff time log. Confirm both baselines during two weeks of observation before the pilot. Guardrail: no refund exceeds what the policy allows.

    Scope

    In: reading emails, checking the policy, calculating amounts, drafting replies for staff review. Out: automatic approval; phone requests. Later: a direct connection to the ticketing platform.

    Assumptions and risks

    Assumptions: The ticketing system can export orders; check a sample export. Staff can review drafts within a day; confirm who owns the queue and their availability. Refund handling takes about 10 staff hours a week and three days per request; check both with two weeks of logs.

    Risks: Emails may lack order details; flag missing information and route them to staff. Special-circumstance requests may be more common than expected; count exceptions during discovery and keep them with a person.

    Recommendation

    Start by measuring two weeks of current refund handling to confirm the baseline, then build the email-reading and policy-check steps first, since they save the most time and are easiest to verify.

    Check your understanding →
    07 / THE TAKEAWAY

    What problem are we actually solving?

    Start with what's actually happening, not what the client asked for. Write the problem in one sentence with a real 'because.' Put numbers on what success looks like, including what must not get worse. Then scope a first version small enough to deliver and check. The best answer won't always be AI-first, and that's part of being a good consultant.

    Check your understanding

    A client says, "We want a chatbot." What's your first move? +

    Ask what's happening today that made them want one. Get the last real example, and find out what problem the chatbot is supposed to solve.

    The goal is "make reporting faster." What's missing? +

    A baseline, a target, a way to measure it, and a guardrail. How long does it take now, how fast should it be, where do the numbers come from, and what can't get worse?

    Why bother writing down what's out of scope? +

    It protects your team's time and sets the client's expectations. When a new request comes up mid-project, you can point to what everyone already agreed on.

    Discovery shows the data you'd need doesn't exist yet. Now what? +

    Make getting the data part of the scope, usually the first part. An AI tool can't use information that isn't recorded anywhere, like Copperline's whiteboard.

    Take this into Week 3: once you know the problem and the goal, you'll learn to write prompts that get reliable, structured output from a model.
