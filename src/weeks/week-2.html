00 / OVERVIEW

Find the real problem. Then scope it.

Turn a vague request into a clear problem, a goal you can measure, and a first version you can actually deliver.

Last week you had a workflow to work with. Clients don't hand you one. They say "we want AI to handle our emails," and it's on you to figure out what's really going wrong and what to build first. Skip that step and you can ship something that works perfectly and helps no one.

Start with the request →
01 / FROM REQUEST TO PROBLEM

The request is a guess.

Clients describe the fix they've imagined. Your job is to find the problem behind it.

We want an AI chatbot for our website.

Staff spend hours answering the same few questions by email.

Can you build us a dashboard?

Leadership doesn't trust the numbers they get today.

Use AI to write our weekly reports.

Reports take two people a full day every week, and half the time goes to collecting data.

Each real problem points somewhere different from the request. A prettier dashboard won't fix numbers nobody trusts.

The problem statement

Once you've found it, pin it down in one sentence:

[Who] struggles with [what] because [root cause], which costs [impact].

If you can't fill in the "because," you're not done with discovery.

02 / DISCOVERY

Ask about what actually happens.

Good discovery gets you real examples, real numbers, and where the data lives.

Discovery is your first real conversation with the client. Don't suggest anything yet. Just learn how the work happens today.

What to ask about

The process today.

Walk me through the last time this happened.

Who does each step?

Volume and time.

How often does this happen in a week?

How long does each one take?

The pain.

What's the most frustrating part?

What does it cost you when it goes wrong?

The data.

Where does this information live today?

Can we get a sample?

Success.

If this were fixed, what would be different?

How would you know?

Limits.

What can't go wrong?

What's your budget, and who would maintain this?

Habits that make discovery work.

  • Ask about the last time, not "usually." Real examples beat general descriptions.
  • Get numbers. "A lot of emails" should become "about 120 a week."
  • Find where the data lives. As you saw in Week 1, a model only knows what's in front of it. Discovery tells you whether that information exists at all.
  • Talk to whoever feels the pain. It's not always the person who called you.
  • Hold off on solutions. Once you pitch, the client stops telling you things.
  • Summarize it back. "So the main problem is… did I get that right?"
03 / DEFINING DONE

"Better" isn't a goal.

If you can't measure it, you can't prove you hit it.

Vague:

Make the weekly report faster.

Clear:

Cut the weekly report from two people's full day to one person's half day within six weeks, measured with a simple time log. Guardrail: every number in the report still matches the source data.

  • Metric: what you measure (time spent on the report).
  • Baseline: where it is today (two people, a full day).
  • Target: where it should land, and by when (one person, half a day, six weeks).
  • How it's measured: where the numbers come from (a time log).
  • Guardrail: what can't get worse (the numbers stay accurate).

The guardrail is the part people skip. It's easy to make anything faster by checking it less, and the guardrail is how you catch that.

Be specific about what you're timing. The wait a customer sees isn't the same as the minutes your staff spend. No baseline yet? Then measuring it is your first task.

04 / SCOPE

Decide what you won't build.

Every meeting adds a "could it also…?" Writing down In, Out, and Later lets you say no without an argument.

In

What the first version does. Keep it small enough to ship and check.

Out

What it won't do, said out loud so nobody assumes otherwise.

Later

Good ideas that wait for a second phase.

Assumptions and risks

Every plan rests on things you're assuming are true. Write them down, with how you'd check each one. Then list what could go wrong and how you'd catch it. When an assumption turns out false, everyone will know why the plan changed.

Common scoping mistakes.

Solving the request, not the problem.

Building exactly what the client asked for.

Vague success.

"Faster" or "better," with no number attached.

Too big a first version.

Trying to automate the whole process at once.

Assuming the data exists.

Finding out mid-project that the information was never written down.

No guardrail.

Hitting the speed target while errors quietly go up.

1 of 7

The request.

The Linden Room, the made-up music venue from Week 1. The owner writes: "Can AI just handle our refund emails? We're drowning."

Discovery.

Three Q&As:

  • "Walk me through the last refund request." → "Someone reads the email, finds the order in our ticketing system, checks the policy, works out the amount, and replies. All by hand."
  • "How many come in, and how long does each take?" → "About 50 a week. Maybe 12 minutes each."
  • "Where do the order details live?" → "In the ticketing platform. I think there's an export button?"

What changes the picture.

Most of those 12 minutes aren't spent writing the reply. They go to finding the order and doing the math. Lookups and math are a job for code; an LLM can read the email and draft the reply.

The problem statement.

Box office staff spend about 10 hours a week on refund emails because every lookup and calculation is done by hand, which delays refunds by days and causes occasional fee mistakes.

What done looks like.

Cut the average time to resolve a refund from 3 days to 1 day within a month, measured from the email timestamp to the refund confirmation. Guardrail: no refund exceeds what the policy allows.

Scope.

  • In: read emails, look up orders, check the policy, calculate amounts, and draft replies for staff to review.
  • Out: automatic approval of any refund; phone requests.
  • Later: a direct connection to the ticketing platform.

Assumptions and risks.

  • Assumptions: the ticketing system can export orders (check a sample export); staff can review drafts within a day.
  • Risks: some emails won't include enough to find the order (flag them for staff); exceptions may be more common than expected (count them, and keep them with a person).

Only reveal these facts when the consultant asks a question that would reasonably lead to them. Never volunteer them:
- About 120 emails a week; more than half ask whether a repair is done.
- Repair status is tracked on a whiteboard in the back, often a day behind.
- Your biggest frustration: mechanics get interrupted constantly.
- Success to you: mechanics aren't interrupted, and customers don't email twice.
- Must never happen: quoting a wrong price (a bad review last year).
- You use Square; budget about $50/month; nobody on staff is technical.

Answer like a busy, friendly shop owner, in 1–3 sentences. If asked about AI models or technical details, say you don't know and that's why you hired them. After the consultant says "end interview," tell them which important facts they never uncovered.

Title

Hearthstone Home Goods: Invoice Matching, Scoping Brief

The request

Hearthstone Home Goods is a made-up home goods retailer. A three-person accounts payable team matches about 800 vendor invoices a month to purchase orders by hand before paying them. The finance director asks: "Can AI just pay our invoices for us?"

The real problem

The accounts payable team spends about 60 hours a month matching invoices because every invoice is checked against its purchase order by hand, which delays vendor payments and led to at least one duplicate payment this year. Assumptions: the hours and the duplicate payment.

Discovery questions

QuestionWhat the answer helps us decide
★ Walk me through the last invoice you matched.Where the time actually goes.
★ How many invoices come in a month, and how long does each take?How big the problem is, and our baseline.
★ Where do invoices and purchase orders live, and can we export them?Whether anything can be automated at all.
What share of invoices don't match on the first try?How much work still needs a person.
What mistakes have happened before?What the guardrail should protect.
Who approves payments today, and above what amount?Which sign-off we have to keep.

What done looks like

MetricBaselineTargetDeadlineHow it's measured
Staff hours on invoice matching~60 hours/month (assumption; confirm with two weeks of time logs)Under 25 hours/monthTwo months after launchTeam time log

Guardrail: no duplicate or overpaid invoices, checked by a monthly audit of every payment.

Scope

InPull fields from invoices, check totals against purchase orders in code, flag duplicates, and send every mismatch to a reviewer. A person still approves every payment.
OutAutomatic payment of any invoice, and invoices that arrive on paper.
LaterPlain-English explanations of mismatches for vendors (Step 5 of the Week 1 Blueprint).

Assumptions and risks

Assumption or riskHow we'd check or catch it
Assumption: invoices arrive as PDFs and purchase orders can be exportedAsk for one sample month of both.

Recommendation

Measure two weeks of current matching to confirm the baseline, then build field extraction and total-checking first, since they take the most time and are the easiest to verify. Before building, confirm that purchase orders can be exported.

Notice that the finance director asked for AI to pay invoices, and the first version doesn't pay anything. That's scoping.

08 / THE TAKEAWAY

What problem are we actually solving?

Start with what's happening, not what was asked for. Write the problem in one sentence with a real "because." Put a number on success, and name what can't get worse. Then scope a first version small enough to ship and check. Sometimes that first version isn't AI at all.

Check your understanding

A client says, "We want a chatbot." What's your first move? +

Ask what's happening today that made them want one. Get the last real example, and find out what problem the chatbot is supposed to solve.

The goal is "make reporting faster." What's missing? +

A baseline, a target, a way to measure it, and a guardrail. How long does it take now, how fast should it be, where do the numbers come from, and what can't get worse?

Why bother writing down what's out of scope? +

It protects your team's time and sets the client's expectations. When a new request comes up mid-project, you can point to what everyone already agreed on.

Discovery shows the data you'd need doesn't exist yet. Now what? +

Make getting the data part of the scope, usually the first part. An AI tool can't use information that isn't recorded anywhere, like Copperline's whiteboard.
