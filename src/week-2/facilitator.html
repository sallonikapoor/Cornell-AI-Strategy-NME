Owner’s fact sheet

Use your own name. You play the owner of Copperline Bikes, a fictional two-location bike shop.

Answer in 1–3 sentences. Never volunteer facts. For technical questions say, “I don’t know, that’s why I hired you.”

Walk me through what happens when an email comes in today.

Whoever's at the counter checks the shared inbox between customers, usually me or Sam. If it's about a repair, we walk to the back and ask the mechanics.

High value for discovery.

How many emails do you get, and what are they about?

About 120 a week. I'd guess more than half are 'is my bike ready yet?' The rest are hours, prices, and the occasional complaint.

High value for discovery.

Where do you keep track of each repair's status?

On a whiteboard in the back. The mechanics update it… usually. Sometimes it's a day behind.

High value for discovery.

What's the most frustrating part of all this?

Every 'is it ready' email means interrupting a mechanic. They hate it, and it slows down repairs. That's honestly what bugs me most.

High value for discovery.

If this were fixed, what would be different?

My mechanics wouldn't get interrupted all day. And customers wouldn't have to email twice because we were slow to answer.

High value for discovery.

Is there anything an answer must never get wrong?

Prices. Last year someone was told $40 for a repair and charged $120. We got a one-star review that still shows up first.

High value for discovery.

What tools do you use, and what's your budget?

We use Square for payments and appointments. I don't want to pay more than about $50 a month for anything new. And nobody here is technical, so it has to be simple.

High value for discovery.

Do you have past emails we could look at?

Sure, everything's in our Gmail going back two years.

High value for discovery.

Which AI model would you like us to use?

I have no idea. Isn't that your job?

Low value for discovery.

Would you like the chatbot to have a fun personality?

Sure, I guess? I just want fewer emails.

Low value for discovery.

Handle phone refund requests.

OUT — The first version covers email requests.

Connect directly to the ticketing platform.

LATER — First prove the process using exported order data.

LATER — Confirm demand and who can review accuracy before expanding languages.

Flag likely fraud.

LATER — Agree on evidence and review rules before adding a new detection task.

Spot the mistake

Moss Laundry: Solving the request, not the problem. The team accepted the requested solution without finding the underlying problem.

Juniper Gym: Vague success. There is no measurable baseline and target.

Pebble Florist: Assuming the data exists. Check where the order data lives before committing to the plan.

Harbor Tutors: No guardrail. Add a check that prevents a faster reply from creating a double booking.

Quiz answers

A client says, "We want a chatbot." What's your first move?

Ask what's happening today that made them want one. Get the last real example, and find out what problem the chatbot is supposed to solve.

The goal is "make reporting faster." What's missing?

A baseline, a target, a way to measure it, and a guardrail. How long does it take now, how fast should it be, where do the numbers come from, and what can't get worse?

Why bother writing down what's out of scope?

It protects your team's time and sets the client's expectations. When a new request comes up mid-project, you can point to what everyone already agreed on.

Discovery shows the data you'd need doesn't exist yet. Now what?

Make getting the data part of the scope, usually the first part. An AI tool can't use information that isn't recorded anywhere, like Copperline's whiteboard.
